E-Grass Power Lab

A working model of my E-Grass System: a power plant that burns elephant grass instead of coal, with a pressure cap that pops like a popcorn kernel to release steam into the turbine. Set the fuel and the cap pressure, watch the plant run, and see how many homes it can power.

Water from the tank fills the container. Burning grass heats it until the steam pressure pops the organic cap. Steam spins the turbine, the turbine spins the magnet, and the wire carries electricity to the homes. The condenser turns steam back into water for the tank.

E-Grass

Why grass is so much lower: the CO₂ released when the grass burns was pulled out of the air while it grew, so it is close to carbon-neutral. The remaining emissions come from farming, drying and transport. The filters in my design clean the exhaust of soot and particles, which protects people's lungs.

Why elephant grass

Fuel comparison

FuelEnergy (dry)Typical moisture

Elephant grass grows fast, about 26 tonnes of dry matter per hectare per year on average, regrows after cutting, and does well on poor land in hot countries. Waste fuels like rice husk and bagasse are limited by how much rice or sugar is produced.

1 · Energy in the fuel

Wet fuel has less usable energy, because some of the heat goes into boiling off its water.

LHV_wet = LHV_dry·(1 − M) − 2.44·M [MJ/kg]

2 · Steam to electricity

Higher steam pressure gives a more efficient steam cycle. This model ranges from about 14% at 5 bar to about 27% at 60 bar, typical for small biomass plants.

kWh/day = tonnes·1000·LHV_wet·η / 3.6

3 · Compared with coal

Coal replaced uses 24 MJ/kg coal at 33% efficiency. CO₂ uses IPCC life-cycle medians: 820 g/kWh for coal, 230 g/kWh for dedicated biomass.

CO₂ avoided = kWh·(820 − 230) g
